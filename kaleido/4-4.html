{4,4} Squares — Tiling Patterns & Symmetry

{4,4} — squares, four to a corner

This is the bathroom floor, and the graph paper, and the chessboard — the pattern most people picture when they hear the word "tiling". Four squares meet at every corner: 4 × 90° = 360°, exactly a turn, which is why it works and why nothing about it feels remarkable until you try the same thing with pentagons.

It is the second of the three regular flat tilings, and the only one that is its own dual — put a dot in each square, join neighbors, and you get squares again.

Its full symmetry is more than it first appears. There are mirrors along both edges of every square, and along both diagonals, which cuts each square into eight identical right triangles. Those eight are the pattern's whole content; everything else is reflections.

Its name is *442:

  • two different 4-fold corners — the center of each square and the point where four squares meet — each bringing the pattern back every quarter turn;
  • a 2-fold corner at the middle of each edge, a half turn.

Two 4-fold corners of different kinds is the interesting part. Rotating about a square's center and rotating about a shared vertex are different symmetries that happen to have the same order, and the notation records both.

The master triangle here has angles of 45°, 45° and 90°. Fold a square of paper in half, in half again, and then along the diagonal, and you are holding it.

Try it: compare this scene with the rectangular kaleidoscope. A diagonal mirror is present here but missing there. Draw a letter or arrow and watch what that one extra mirror does to it.
